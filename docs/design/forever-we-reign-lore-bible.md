# Forever We Reign: original lore bible

**Version:** proposal 1, 2026-09-30.
**Authority:** The operator approved the title, a soldier fighting through bosses to regain his throne, the discovery that the throne is cursed, and a sequel hook. He subsequently requested a creative agent to develop wholly original lore with research behind it.
**Status:** The premise is approved. Every new name, institution, historical detail, dialogue line, curse mechanism, item name, and sequel direction below is an authored proposal. This is a complete writing foundation, not implemented content or approval of new production scope.

## The story in one paragraph

Iven Sarr, a soldier chosen to rule Aulden Reach, returns from exile to reclaim the Anchor Seat above a storm-battered coast. Four former allies bar his ascent, each defending a different promise the kingdom made to its people. Iven believes the weather engine has become a weapon in their hands. By winning their duels, he restores access and proves his claim. When he finally sits, the throne binds him to every unfulfilled vow spoken by the rulers before him. Their preserved wills begin issuing orders through his own voice. He has regained his throne and inherited a curse that makes leaving the office harder than taking it.

The campaign asks: **Who does a throne belong to when everyone has been promised its protection?** The answer remains incomplete at the ending. The soldier wins his kingdom; the next story can ask how he frees it without becoming another ruler who makes someone else pay for his victory.

## Setting: Aulden Reach

Aulden Reach is a coastal kingdom built around the ability to survive the weather. Ceramic channels collect charge. Brass conduits carry it uphill. Dark-stone gates divide settlements from the sea. Luminous glass records voices and pressure changes; cloth markings identify the crews who maintain each mechanism.

The coast was once repeatedly devastated by seasonal storms. The weather engine made steady harbors and reliable growing seasons possible. Its governors gradually stopped describing themselves as stewards and began claiming that only their uninterrupted rule kept the world safe.

The engine can divert and delay storms within a finite catchment. It cannot remove weather from existence. Promising permanent protection without admitting that limit created debts of risk, maintenance, and displacement. The supernatural curse gives those deferred obligations a will of their own.

The engine is part machine, part oath-working. Materials and measured maintenance matter. Magic never means a convenient new rule that invalidates an enemy tell, rerolls equipment, or excuses a broken save.

That distinction makes the final discovery coherent: he is reclaiming his throne, not discovering a curse he already experienced at a previous binding ceremony.

Dates are relative writing references, not a new calendar simulation. The catastrophe was local, not a claim that this entire fictional world flooded.

## People and institutions

### Iven Sarr, the displaced soldier-king

Iven earned authority by keeping supply lines open and bringing wounded people home. He still speaks and dresses like an officer. He regards hesitation as an invitation for someone else to suffer, which makes him brave in a duel and dangerous when asked to make an absolute promise.

His starting belief is that reclaiming a legitimate office will restore order. His journey shows that every opponent can name people whom his restoration might endanger. He does not learn that his entire life was false; he learns that a true claim can carry an impossible obligation.

His visual identity stays readable across equipment: a narrow repaired cloth service sash, one ceramic plate bearing his company mark, and an asymmetrical shoulder closure. None grants a hidden bloodline power. The final curse marks his authority, not his ancestry.

### Kes Orra, keeper of the refuge

Kes maintains the return hoist, repairs armor, and records the names of people rescued along the coast. She supported Iven's appointment but refuses to call any ruler indispensable. Her conversations make the stakes concrete: a repaired water gate, a missing crew, a home that can be reached again.

She can appear through the existing refuge interface and a small number of authored lines. This proposal does not require another combat rig, companion AI, romance system, or branching dialogue engine.

Their disagreements explain enemies and routes. They are not three currency tracks or reputation grinds. Regular guards may be frightened, dutiful, or self-serving without requiring a bespoke biography for every encounter.

## The two regions

### Region one: Lowwater Works

The approved coastal machinery and foundry district becomes Lowwater Works. Its question is practical: who gets protection when the gates cannot save everyone?

Routes reuse the same kit. A foundry route favors compressed interiors and machinery; a seawall route favors open pressure gates and salt-worn structures. Optional cache or narrative nodes reveal service records and equipment patterns. Two mandatory boss nodes remain the region's only boss budget.

Foreshadowing: high flood lines above an official "safe" mark, a gate repaired with mismatched ceramic, and a ledger that records protection for a street no longer on the current map. No clue names the curse yet.

### Region two: The Highglass Array

The approved elevated observatory and weather-engine complex becomes the Highglass Array. Its question is institutional: who has the right to decide which promises count?

Routes use maintenance stairs, observation galleries, shutter mechanisms, and the same engine architecture. The third boss protects the archive; the fourth controls the final seat chamber. The throne reveal occurs here without a new environment kit.

Foreshadowing: older decrees underneath fresh ones, instruments whose labels disagree with their readings, and a maintenance note describing a ruler as a "continuing guarantor." Characters initially understand this as legal language. Its literal meaning arrives at the ending.

### Ending treatment

For the first time, the rain stops over Lowwater. Iven gives a modest order: open the return hoist so the stranded crews can come home. The engine instead begins sealing the gates. Several preserved voices use his mouth to recite contradictory orders that once protected different districts.

He stands. The chorus does not stop. Fine luminous lines remain beneath the skin of his wrist, matching the channels in the empty throne. Sitting was the recognition of the guarantor, not the limit of the curse's reach.

Iven hears Kes on the chamber speaking tube. He cannot yet explain what happened, but he refuses the order the engine wants him to issue. He tells her to keep the refuge open. A distant receiver lights up with an old boundary designation that is absent from the current map.

The final image is a recovered throne standing empty while the king continues to hear it. End on his unresolved responsibility. Do not solve the curse, announce a hidden family villain, undo the campaign, or introduce another fight after the fourth boss.

## Curse rules for writers

1. The engine stores witnessed promises and the recorded intent of those who authorized them. The voices are preserved institutional wills, not evidence that every dead person can be summoned.
2. Its original rule requires a living sovereign to guarantee obligations that their office accepted. Later administrations concealed conflicts rather than discharging them.
3. Recognition binds Iven because his claim is valid. The curse is not a punishment for secretly being the wrong heir.
4. The binding presses him toward keeping all inherited promises at once. Those promises are incompatible. It can distort speech and engine commands; it does not arbitrarily rewrite combat inputs or saved ownership.
5. Standing up, breaking the chair, killing a rival, or changing equipment cannot erase obligations. Physical dismantling would also threaten the functioning weather network.
6. A possible answer must distinguish justified duties from coerced or impossible promises. The first game's ending does not supply that answer.

Before the reveal, writers may show omitted costs and contradictory records. They may not have an NPC fully explain these six rules to Iven. Edrast knows that continuity consumes its bearer; he does not need a complete secret cosmology.

## Defeat, rebirth, and replay without continuity cheats

The existing reconstruction fiction remains a writing proposal. Recommended treatment: the refuge repairs Iven's ceramic protection and retrieves him through its return network after a failed expedition. Recovery and renewed preparation are the first meaning of "rebirth." A lost attempt does not create a new son, bloodline, clone collection, or permanent story death.

Subsequent difficulty tiers can be framed as **Reckonings**, re-enactments assembled from the campaign's preserved combat and route records. They repeat known confrontations to test readiness after the revelation. They do not resurrect four dead bosses or repeatedly bind Iven to a new curse.

The canonical first campaign happens once. Retry presentation before completion preserves that campaign's unresolved progress; later challenge presentation labels its retelling. First-clear journal facts, unique narrative acknowledgments, and ending discovery are permanent. Replaying a reveal is viewing a record, not performing another historical coronation.

This is fiction for the approved loop and bounded tiers. It does not require a new simulation system. Save and checkpoint behavior continues to follow the existing progression contract.

## Collection fiction: patterns rather than repeated stolen relics

Earned caches contain preserved manufacturing patterns and usable equipment prepared by prior crews. The refuge can recover or reproduce those patterns. A dramatic reveal shows the equipment already granted by the committed reward transaction; the reveal does not decide what the player receives.

Boss first clears acknowledge a specific service, record, or token once. Later equipment copies use the associated pattern. A second Crownlink does not mean the original throne component was torn out again. A duplicate is an owned pattern, converted to ordinary currency under the existing design, not an extra unique artifact or another mastery award.

The six talismans are the launch's relic-like objects. "Relic" is flavor, not a seventh equipment category. Future weapon finishes or commemorative variants can follow this manufacturing fiction, but their implementation and catalog budget require separate scope. Launch visuals remain attached to the existing twelve weapon definitions.

### Reward and lore integrity rules

- Region pools express the crews and institutions encountered there, using the existing 34 definitions rather than additional faction items.
- Fixed first-clear choices preserve the existing choice across melee families. A boss's enemy-only weapon does not override that contract.
- Journal context never depends on winning a low-probability draw. Mandatory story information arrives at authored campaign beats.
- Direct purchases are commissioned reproductions of known patterns. Ordinary currency remains the sole normal acquisition and upgrade currency.
- Displayed probabilities, duplicate conversion, protection, and completion stay explicit. The lore does not hide odds behind a prophecy or treat disappointing draws as the player's fate.
- Unique story claims and equipment grants retain independent, stable identities. Reward replay cannot repeat an original historical acknowledgment.
- Future earned collections can preserve memories of places or services. Recruiting random souls or playable heroes would require a separately approved game system.

## Sequel room, without a sequel production commitment

Recommended continuation: Iven must separate protection from permanent personal rule. The old designation on the receiver suggests that the engine's current map erased communities still bound by its promises. A later game could explore those obligations and other nodes of the same network, with Iven challenged by conflicting preserved wills.

This permits new authored places, equipment traditions, and rivals without inventing a new apocalypse every installment. It also permits a successor story if Iven's office is eventually relinquished. Neither direction establishes a release date, extra launch geography, online service, paid gacha, or a second protagonist system now.

The unresolved question is whether the kingdom can preserve mutual duties without forcing one person to reign forever. The eventual answer should cost effort and restitution, not a larger weapon number.

## Writing and art handoff

Launch writing should be sparse: one refuge opening, four boss introductions and defeat exchanges, a small set of optional records, and the final throne reveal. Record counts and scene durations must fit the corresponding content tasks; this bible does not enlarge their budgets.

Keep mandatory lines concrete and readable on a phone. Optional entries can describe who built a tool or why a gate was altered. No codex entry needs a second cosmology to explain a sword.

Character and weapon art should start with functional silhouette, grip, contact, weight, and portrait visibility. Ceramic and brass surface refinement follows validated movement. The README concept image is a visual target, not evidence that the current untextured prototype reaches that quality.

Before committing finished lore assets, review these proposed names and the curse treatment against the approved premise. Physical combat qualification, weapon-family validation, and camera acceptance remain separate from narrative approval.
